Finding the hypothesis that does the work

A recent relations proof made the role of an assumption unusually clear.

Danny Perojevic · October 13, 2026· Updated October 15, 2026

The claim I had to check

Does a symmetric, transitive relation have to be reflexive? No. On A = {1, 2}, take R = {(1, 1)}. This relation is symmetric and transitive, but (2, 2) is missing. Neither property creates a pair involving an element that never appears.

Add the domain condition

Now suppose Dom(R) = A as well. Choose an arbitrary a in A. The domain condition gives some b with aRb. Symmetry gives bRa. Transitivity combines aRb and bRa to give aRa. Since a was arbitrary, R is reflexive on A.

Where each assumption went

This proof is short enough that I can point to the exact job of each hypothesis. The domain condition supplies a pair. Symmetry reverses it. Transitivity produces the loop. Without the first step, there may be nothing to reverse. The counterexample makes that gap visible.

What I want to remember

When I get stuck, I can ask which assumption I have not used and what it actually supplies. That is more useful than trying to guess the next line from how a previous proof looked. Here, the entire argument starts with finding the partner b.
