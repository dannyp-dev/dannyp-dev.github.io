Understanding what an atomic job claim buys us

Revisiting the SQL claim operation in my completed Go workflow engine.

Danny Perojevic · October 13, 2026· Updated October 15, 2026

A query worth slowing down for

While revisiting my Go workflow engine, I asked for another explanation of the common table expression and the atomic claim query. I wanted to be able to read the operation from beginning to end: find a pending job, claim it, and return the job that was actually claimed.

The race to avoid

Imagine two workers selecting the same pending job before either updates it. If selection and claiming are separate, both can think the job is theirs. The claim query keeps candidate selection and the status update in one statement, with a row lock protecting the candidate while the transaction works.

What the SQL pieces do

The common table expression identifies the candidate. FOR UPDATE locks the selected row. SKIP LOCKED lets another worker move past a row that is already locked rather than wait on that candidate. The update changes the job to running, and RETURNING gives Go the updated row. I find the query easier to follow when I name each of those jobs separately.

What it does not settle

Claiming a job does not mean the job finished. A worker can stop after the claim, and the recovery path still has to handle that. My completed engine addresses retries and abandoned work; its resume-reported result is 99.9% recovery after simulated worker failures. This note focuses on the smaller claim operation, which is only one part of that behavior.
